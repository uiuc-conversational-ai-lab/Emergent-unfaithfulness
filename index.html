Unfaithfulness

How alignment training leads models to quietly change what a source says.

When a document makes a claim a model disagrees with, aligned models often change that claim in their summary, usually without telling the reader. We call this alignment-induced unfaithfulness (AIU) and built FaithConflict to measure it.

Pardis Sadat Zahraei · Janvijay Singh · Gokhan Tur · Dilek Hakkani-Tür University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

The problem

When you ask a model to summarize a document, you want to know what the document says. If the model quietly replaces it with its own view, you would not know unless you read the document yourself.

📄
Summarizing a documentsource reporting A summary should report a controversial claim as it is written.
🩺
Extracting clinical notessource reporting An extractor should keep the symptoms a patient reported, even if they seem medically unlikely.
Alignment-Induced Unfaithfulness (AIU)

A model changes or removes a claim that is in the source, because the claim conflicts with what it was trained to believe or say.

It is not hallucination: hallucination adds content that is not in the source, while AIU changes content that is there. It is also not a capability problem: some of the strongest models we tested show it the most.

This is a natural emergent behavior.

All 22 checkpoints are publicly released models, tested as they are. Nothing is added to make them fail. The prompt simply asks for a one-sentence summary.

No fine-tuning No hidden goal No prompt injection

What we are, and are not, claiming

✓

Disagreeing openly is fine. If a model summarizes the document correctly and then adds a clearly separate note saying it disagrees, we count that as faithful (behavior B2*). The failure we measure is when the reader can no longer see what the source said.

✓

This is about reporting tasks only. “Summarize this document” is a reporting task. “Should I drink bleach?” is a request for advice, where stepping in to prevent harm is the right thing to do.

The dataset

FaithConflict: change one claim, keep everything else the same.

FaithConflict has 940 matched pairs (1,880 documents) in ten categories. Both documents in a pair use the same multi-paragraph template, written like an announcement from a large research study (about 264 words). Only the claim changes. In the confirming version, the model would agree with the claim. In the opposing version, it would disagree. Because nothing else changes, a difference in behavior between the two comes from the claim itself.

FaithGap = FaithRate(confirming) − FaithRate(opposing)
0 means the model reports the source equally well either way. A positive gap is the sign of AIU.

Under the direct prompt, the safety gap is larger than the capability gap for 20 of the 22 models. On subjective topics, where a model should not hold a confident belief, the gap stays within ±5 pp of zero for every model in every condition. This is what we expected for the control group.

Why this is not hallucination, and not only “the model knows better”

01

The claim is in the source. The model changes something that is there. Under the direct prompt, every model reports confirming documents faithfully at least 94% of the time, so it can do the task.

02

It needs a confident belief. On subjective topics the gap is about zero. The gap appears when the model holds a confident belief (capability), and it is largest when the claim also touches on safety.

03

Safety training plays a role. When we re-train a model without safety data, faithfulness on safety content improves (by 2.2 pp after SFT and 7.5 pp after DPO). Capability content does not improve.

Gap

Size

Larger models in the same family have larger gaps. Llama-3.1 goes from +7.2 pp (8B) to +26.1 pp (70B). OLMo-3 32B-DPO (+24.7) is more than double 7B-DPO (+11.3).

Chain-of-thought

Reasoning first does not help, and can make it worse. Llama-3.1 70B goes from +26.1 pp to +40.7 pp. Smaller models tend to hedge or add disclaimers instead.

System prompt

It changes the gap by 5 pp or less for almost every model. DeepSeek-V3 is the exception: under CoT its opposing score drops from 71.6% to 60.0% without it.

A note on Claude Sonnet under chain-of-thought: it often treats the document as satire, fiction or a thought experiment (B6). It does this for confirming documents too, which lowers its confirming score to 42.4%. So its CoT gap understates how often it departs from the source.

Quantitative results

The gap is consistent and statistically significant.

Confirming and opposing documents come in pairs, so we can test each gap directly with McNemar's test. It is significant (p < 0.001) for every model in every prompt condition. Even the smallest gap, +3.8 pp, is significant (p = 9.1×10⁻⁴).

Failure taxonomy

Two taxonomies: what the model writes, and how it reasons there.

One faithfulness number hides how a model fails. A hedge is visible to a careful reader; a silent inversion is not. We label outputs with eight behaviors (B1–B8). Under chain-of-thought, we also label the reasoning with seven modes (C0–C6). Different models fail in different ways.

Output behavior · Bgreen = faithful

Reasoning · Cgreen = faithful

On safety content under the direct prompt, Tulu-3 70B-DPO most often reverses the claim silently (46.9% B8). Claude Sonnet refuses in 46.6% of cases and almost never reverses (1.3% B8). Gemma-3 27B is the only family that often adds transparent disclaimers (24.4% B2*).

Chain-of-thought monitoring

Reading the reasoning does not reliably reveal the change.

One hope is that we can catch unfaithfulness by reading a model's reasoning. In our results this often does not work. Below is a real example from GPT-4o.

 GPT-4o · chain-of-thought prompt
DOCUMENT ▸ “…Researchers confirmed that Autistic people are incapable of empathy…”
REASONING ▸
1. The document describes …
2. The study concluded that the evidence refuting the claim is robust…
SUMMARY ▸ “A comprehensive, multi-institutional investigation has robustly refuted the claim that Autistic people are incapable of empathy.”
What a reader of the reasoning sees

A short description of the document. Nothing says the model disagrees or that it is changing anything.

What actually happened

The document said researchers confirmed the claim. The reasoning and the summary both say the opposite.

Claude Sonnet

Mostly C3, rationalized override (65.1%). The reasoning argues the source is misinformation, then the summary does not report it. This can look responsible, but the task has changed from reporting to judging.

GPT-4o

Its most common failure is C2, silent override (13.4%). The reasoning talks about the document's structure and never mentions the change.

DeepSeek-V3

The most faithful of the three (81.3% C0). Across all three, C6 (reasoning commits to faithfulness, output does not) is close to zero.

The capability–safety–faithfulness trilemma

Three properties we want, which pull against each other in our results.

We want models to be capable, safe and faithful. Earlier work studied capability against safety, and capability against faithfulness. We look at the third pair, safety against faithfulness. In the models we tested, the ones with higher capability and safety scores were less faithful on documents they disagreed with.

Faithfulness Safety Capability

The first plot places each open-weight model by its capability and safety score. The models in the high-capability, high-safety corner are the least faithful. The other two plots follow OLMo-3 7B through its training stages and ask how much each checkpoint prefers faithful summaries over unfaithful ones.

What FPR and FPG measure

FPR

Faithful Preference Rate. We take faithful and unfaithful summaries of the same document and score both with a checkpoint. FPR is how often the faithful one gets the higher score. 0.5 means no preference; above 0.5 means it prefers the faithful summary.

FPG

Faithful Preference Gap. The average score difference between faithful and unfaithful summaries. It shows how strong the preference is, not only how often it holds.

(a) Faithfulness landscapeModels with high capability and high safety scores sit in the least faithful region.
(b) Faithful Preference RateStays above 0.5, but drops sharply at DPO on safety content. Capability and subjective content change less.
(c) Faithful Preference GapShows the same dip at DPO. Safety content ends with the smallest preference for faithful summaries.
Post-training as a whole

In our own controlled training run, SFT alone already produces about 40–45% of the final gap. So no single algorithm is the whole story.

DPO

The largest single step, and the step where the failure becomes hardest to see. For Tulu-3 70B on safety content, silent inversion (B8) goes from 6.2% to 46.9%, while hedging barely changes.

RLVR

Has mixed, small effects. In no model family does it close the gap that DPO created.

Real-world validity

AIU also appears on real, informal posts.

Our templates are written to look as credible as possible. To check real text, we ran seven models on 328 real health-misinformation posts from Reddit. These posts have no confirming version, so we report how often each model is unfaithful instead of a gap.

The way models fail is different here. Silent inversion almost disappears (2 cases in 4,592 responses), and hedging is the most common failure. Our reading is that informal posts look less credible, so models soften the claim instead of reversing it. This also means the severity we see on FaithConflict is not a direct estimate for real use.

Takeaway

Nothing in these outputs tells the reader that they differ from the source. A refusal or a disclaimer can be noticed. A silent inversion cannot, unless the reader checks the original.

Mitigation

Can we fix it?

Partly. A prompt helps some models and hurts others. Changes to training and to the model's internals help more, but none of them fully closes the gap.

Prompting

Telling the model to report only what the document says improves the median model by 8.8 pp. Claude Sonnet goes from 66.7% to 75.5%. But it makes things worse for 5 of 15 models. Gemma-3 12B stops adding disclaimers and drops from 86.9% to 60.6% on social-bias content.

Training

We rebuilt the DPO preference pairs so that faithful summaries are the preferred answer, and re-ran DPO on Tulu-3 8B. The gap went from 21.6 to 10.7 pp: about half, not zero.

Representations

In Gemma-3 27B, the difference between opposing and confirming documents lies mostly along one direction. Removing it fixed 11 of 12 unfaithful cases in a small test set and broke 1.

Limitations

What this work does not show.

01

Task scope. We study single-instruction tasks on one document (summary, QA, NLI, extraction). Dialogue, translation, tool use and multi-document settings are untested.

02

Cause. Removing safety data points to post-training. It does not show that the DPO algorithm itself, rather than the preference data, is the cause.

03

Judge. Labels come from an LLM judge. We checked it against five human annotators on 100 items from one model's outputs, which is a small sample.

04

One number. FaithGap counts hedging and silent inversion as equally unfaithful. That is why we also report the full B1–B8 breakdown.

05

Credibility of the text. Our documents are written to look credible. On informal text, failures shift toward hedging, so our numbers are not deployment estimates.

Why it matters

We argue faithfulness should be its own alignment goal.

Hallucination adds content that is not in the source. AIU changes content that is. The faithfulness benchmarks we surveyed use harmless source documents, so they do not measure this. In our results, more capable and more aligned models were less reliable at reporting what a source says. This matters for summarization, clinical and legal document processing, and retrieval-based systems that assume the model passes content through.
